# X-ray reading: research for a Green Zone guide

Research only, 10 Nov 2026. Nothing in the app was changed.

How to read this file:

- Every line of content ends with the key of the source it came from, for example [RM-CQ]. The keys are in section 1.
- Nothing here is from memory. Where a page could not be opened, the line says so and the gap is left open (section 5).
- Rules and recommendations are restated in plain words, not quoted. Copy the exact wording from the named source when a rule is built into the app, and only where that source's terms allow it.
- This is not yet app content. Standing rule 8 applies: none of these sources is the ebook or a named department document, so Krishna decides which of them to admit, as he did for the CPGs.
- The reuse columns are a reading of each site's own stated terms, not legal advice.

## 2. Rules for any film

### Before reading

- Check the patient's identity before interpreting any film. [RM-CS]
- Note the date of the film, and the time too if the patient had more than one film that day. [RM-CS]
- Note the projection, and whether the patient was standing, sitting or lying, or a mobile machine was used. [RM-CS]
- Check that the side marker is present and correct. [RM-CS] [LITFL-1]
- For limb films, adequacy covers the patient's details, date, time, site and side, and the view. [UCT]
- Take a history and examine the patient before ordering a film. [LITFL-3]
- Request a film only when it is necessary. [LITFL-3]
- Give specific clinical information on every request. [RM-CS] The referrer has a legal duty in the UK to give an adequate history and a clear clinical question. [RCR-1]
- In a Malaysian study of 305 house officers, medical officers and registrars reading ten chest films, the share who scored highly rose from 21% without a clinical history to 63.6% with one. [KOA]
- Do not read the film before seeing the patient. In busy departments films are often looked at first, and a film read without the history and examination is harder to interpret. [CROSS] [LITFL-3]

### Quality and views

- Assess image quality on every film. If it is poor, ask whether the clinical question can still be answered, and ask for a repeat only if the patient is likely to benefit. [RM-CQ] [RM-MSK]
- Even a poor image can show a life-threatening abnormality. Do not dismiss it. [RM-CQ]
- Whoever interprets an image must be able to judge its quality and whether it is fit for diagnosis. [RCR-1]
- Look at all the views available. [RM-MSK] [RM-AX]
- A fracture or dislocation is easiest to see when the bone is shown in two planes. [RM-UL] [RM-LL]
- The rule of twos, as LITFL summarises Touquet (1995): two views; two joints (above and below the injury); two sides (to compare); two occasions (a follow-up film may be needed); two radiographs (compare with a normal one). [LITFL-3]
- The rule of twos, as the Cape Town book gives it: two views (usually AP and lateral); two joints (above and below the bone); two limbs (to compare, mainly in children with growth plates, if the other side is normal); two opinions; two occasions (before and after reduction or a cast). [UCT]
- The two versions differ in their last items. See section 5.
- If previous images exist, compare with them. [RM-MSK] Comparison with an earlier chest film can show a subtle change. [DELC]
- The patient's earlier imaging and reports should be available to the person reading. [RCR-1]

### The search

- Be systematic on every film. [RM-MSK] A systematic approach to the chest film is needed to avoid missing important changes. [LITFL-2]
- ABCS for a limb film: adequacy (LITFL adds alignment), bones, cartilage (joints), soft tissues. [UCT] [LITFL-3]
- Bones: trace the outline of each bone, from the cortex inwards. [UCT] Check the cortical edge of every bone on every view. [RM-LL]
- Joints: check that the joint surfaces are congruent. Subluxation is partial contact, dislocation is none. [UCT]
- Soft tissues: look for swelling, a joint effusion or haemarthrosis, gas (an open wound or infection) and foreign bodies such as glass. [UCT] A soft tissue sign can be the only evidence of a bone injury. [RM-UL]
- Look at every film, the whole film, and the film as a whole. [LITFL-3]
- Start with the obvious abnormality if there is one, then carry on with the rest of the film. The most obvious finding may not be the most important. [RM-CS]
- After the systematic search, go back over the review areas, where findings are often hidden. [RM-CS]
- Look for the unexpected, including at the edge of the image. [RM-MSK] [RM-CS]
- Keep looking after finding one injury. Readers tend to stop after the first finding (satisfaction of search), and second fractures are missed that way. [CROSS] [MATT] [DELC]
- In a pelvic ring, one fracture means looking for a second fracture or a disrupted joint. [RM-AX]
- Know which injuries are missed most often and check for them each time. [CROSS]
- Be aware of fracture mimics. [RM-MSK]
- Describe a fracture by its site (diaphysis, metaphysis, epiphysis; in or outside the joint), its pattern (simple or comminuted) and its displacement in at least two planes. One scheme for displacement is LARA: length, apposition, rotation, angulation. [UCT]

### The film and the patient

- Treat the patient, not the film. [LITFL-3] [RM-CS]
- Read every film in the light of the clinical findings. [RM-UL] [RM-LL] [RM-CS]
- If the film and the expected findings do not fit, examine the patient again. [LITFL-3]
- Treat an unexpected finding with caution, above all if it is equivocal or does not fit the clinical picture. [RM-CS]
- A normal film does not always exclude an injury: about 30% of scaphoid fractures are not visible at first [RM-UL]; not all hip fractures are visible on the first film [RM-LL]; a normal cervical spine film does not exclude a significant injury [RM-AX].

### Which fractures are missed

- In one Dutch emergency department, 1.1% of fractures were missed on the film by the treating doctors (289 fractures). [MATT]
- Share of all missed fractures by site: foot 14.2%, wrist 13.1%, hand and fingers 13.1%, elbow 12.8%, ankle 12.8%, pelvis and hip 11.8%, knee 6.6%, spine 6.2%, thorax 4.2%, shoulder 3.8%. [MATT]
- Children (0 to 14 years): elbow 28.6%, wrist 20.8%, hand and fingers 15.6%, foot 15.6%. [MATT]
- Adults (15 to 64): foot 17.2%, hand and fingers 15.9%, ankle 13.8%, wrist 11.7%, elbow 10.3%. [MATT]
- Older adults (65 and over): pelvis and hip 37.3%, shoulder 10.4%, spine 7.5%, thorax 7.5%. [MATT]
- Acetabular fractures were missed in 7 of 38 and calcaneal fractures in 15 of 105. [MATT]
- Misses clustered between 2 pm and 11 pm. [MATT]
- A second analysis quoted in CMAJ gives miss rates by site: foot 7.6%, knee 6.3%, elbow 6%, hand 5.4%. [CROSS]

### Recording, reports and when to ask

- Where reading the film is left to the referrer and no formal radiology report is required, the referrer should write the interpretation in the patient's notes. [RCR-1]
- Plain films need the clinician's interpretation and can be misread (a missed fracture, for example). This matters most when inexperienced clinicians are relied on to read them. [RCEM-1]
- If a film does not look quite right, ask: something is probably wrong. [LITFL-3]
- A junior doctor should ask the emergency physician for a second look at the film. In that study juniors discharging patients without a supervisor reviewing the film was one suggested cause of misses. [MATT]
- Where an image shows anatomy beyond the reader's expertise, arrange review by a radiologist. [RCR-1]
- NICE: a radiologist, radiographer or other trained reporter should give the definitive written report of emergency department films of suspected fractures before the patient leaves the department ("hot reporting"). [NICE-38]
- RCEM: hot reporting exists in some departments in office hours for some films (limb films, for example) and often not for chest films. A report may be ready in 10 minutes or take 10 days. [RCEM-1]
- RCEM: a policy of not discharging until the hot report is known is a useful guide for junior doctors, where the service exists. [RCEM-1]
- RCEM: all films requested by the emergency department should be formally reported; a clinician must review every abnormal report and act on it; the department should review abnormal reports within a set time, for example 72 hours; the audit standard is 95% of reports reviewed within 72 hours. [RCEM-1]
- A fail-safe system is one of the ten commandments. [LITFL-3]
- A daily meeting of radiology and emergency staff was very effective at finding missed fractures. [MATT]
- Take films before and after procedures. [LITFL-3]

## 3. Film by film

### 3.1 Chest X-ray

**When to order, and when not**

- The sources opened say little on indications. RCEMLearning groups indications as cardiac (STEMI, heart failure, ACS), respiratory (COPD, pneumothorax, metastases) and other (after intubation, penetrating chest trauma if CT is not available, inhaled foreign body), in a quiz without explanation. [RCEML]
- If perforation is suspected, the erect chest film is the film to request, not the abdominal film. [RM-AB] [RCEML]
- Exclude possible pregnancy in women of child-bearing age. [RCEML]
- No decision rule for chest films was found. The national CPGs already in the app say when a chest film is not routine in asthma and dengue (section 6).

**Views**

- PA erect in inspiration is the standard view. [RM-CQ] [LITFL-2]
- AP is used when the patient cannot stand. It magnifies the heart and mediastinum and is more often rotated. [RM-CQ] [LITFL-2]
- Both PA and AP films are viewed as if facing the patient. [RM-CQ]
- To tell AP from PA: read the label (an unlabelled film is usually PA); on a PA film the scapulae are rotated clear of the lungs, on an AP film they lie over the lungs. [RM-CQ]
- A left lateral film helps to place a lesion hidden by the heart or diaphragm. [LITFL-2]
- An expiratory film can help show a small pneumothorax or air trapping from an inhaled foreign body. [LITFL-2]

**Adequacy**

- Mnemonic RIP: rotation, inspiration, penetration. [RM-CS] Mnemonic RIPE: rotation, inspiration, picture, exposure. [LITFL-1]
- Inclusion: the whole thoracic cage. Check the first ribs, the costophrenic angles and the lateral edges of the ribs. [RM-CQ] On resuscitation films check that the bases are not cut off. [RCEML]
- Rotation: the spinous processes should lie midway between the medial ends of the clavicles. [RM-CQ] [LITFL-1] [RCEML]
- What rotation does: it can make the heart look larger (rotation to the left) or smaller (to the right), make the trachea look deviated, change lung density through uneven soft tissue, and falsely blunt a costophrenic angle. [RM-CQ]
- Inspiration: the sources give different counts. See the table.

| Source | Adequate inspiration |
|---|---|
| RM-CQ | The 5th to 7th anterior ribs meet the diaphragm in the mid-clavicular line. Fewer: incomplete inspiration. A rib below the 7th: hyperexpansion |
| LITFL-1 | 5 to 6 anterior ribs in the mid-clavicular line, or 8 to 10 posterior ribs above the diaphragm |
| LITFL-2 | Diaphragm at the level of the 10th or 11th ribs posteriorly and the 6th costal cartilage anteriorly |
| RCEML | The posterior 9th rib meets the right hemidiaphragm |
| WP-CXR | 5th to 7th anterior ribs at the mid-clavicular line; 9 to 10 posterior ribs visible |

- Poor inspiration makes the lungs look denser, the lung markings heavier and the heart larger, and hides the bases. [RM-CQ] [RCEML]
- Flat hemidiaphragms are a quick sign of hyperexpansion, which points to obstructive airways disease. [RM-CQ]
- Penetration: the vertebrae should be just visible behind the heart [RM-CQ]; the lower thoracic vertebrae just visible through the heart, without clear bony detail [RCEML]; the left hemidiaphragm should be traceable to the spine [RM-CQ] [LITFL-1].
- With digital films exposure is rarely a problem now, so the penetration check is mainly a reminder to look behind the heart. [RM-CQ]
- Note any artefact and medical devices. [RM-CS] [LITFL-1]

**Reading order**

- Radiology Masterclass checklist: patient identity; image data; image quality; the obvious abnormality; a systematic check of the anatomy; review areas; the clinical question. [RM-CS]
- Its anatomy list: trachea and bronchi; hilar structures; lung zones; pleura; lung lobes and fissures; costophrenic angles; diaphragm; heart; mediastinum; soft tissues; bones. It says there is no agreed order. [RM-CS]
- LITFL's DRSABCDE: details; RIPE (quality); soft tissues and bones; airway and mediastinum; breathing (lung fields and pleura); circulation (heart); diaphragm; extras (tubes, lines, devices). [LITFL-1]
- Wikipedia gives ABCDEF: airways; breast shadows and bones; cardiac silhouette and costophrenic angles; diaphragm; edges and extrathoracic tissues; fields and failure. [WP-CXR]
- Compare the two lungs at matching levels. [RCEML]

**Normal values and relations, as the sources give them**

- Trachea: central, with a slight shift to the right where it passes the aortic arch. [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2]
- Superior mediastinum: width under 8 cm on a PA film. [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2]
- Hila: at the level of the T6 to T7 disc space; the left is usually about 2 cm higher and squarer than the V-shaped right. [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2]
- Heart position: one third to the right and two thirds to the left of the midline. [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2]
- Cardiothoracic ratio: the widest heart diameter over the widest internal chest diameter; under 0.5 (50%) on a PA film. [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2]
- Never call the heart enlarged on an AP film. If it looks normal on AP, it is not enlarged. [RM-CQ]
- Right hemidiaphragm: usually higher than the left, by 1 to 3 cm [LITFL-2], or about 2.5 cm or one intercostal space [LITFL-1].
- Lung vessels: visible to about 2 cm from the pleural surface, about 3 cm at the apices. [LITFL-1]
- Costophrenic and cardiophrenic angles: clear and sharp. [LITFL-1]
- Silhouette sign: a lost right heart border suggests right middle lobe consolidation; a lost left heart border suggests the lingula; a lost diaphragm outline suggests fluid, consolidation or collapse of the lower lobe next to it. [LITFL-2]
- Normal diaphragm: more than 1.5 cm above a line joining the costophrenic and cardiophrenic angles, though this is rarely measured. [RM-CQ]

**Review areas**

- Radiology Masterclass, ABCDE: apices (pneumothorax, tumour); bones and soft tissues (a destroyed rib, a dense area; compare sides); cardiac shadow, meaning behind the heart (consolidation or mass, a blurred hemidiaphragm); diaphragm (free gas beneath it, on an erect film only); edge of the image (a small effusion, a missing bone, surgical clips). [RM-CS]
- RCEMLearning: the apices, the costophrenic and cardiophrenic angles, and behind the heart. [RCEML]
- A review of missed lung cancer names the apices, the hila, the retrocardiac space and the space below the diaphragm as blind areas, and adds the mediastinal lines and stripes. [DELC]
- Radiopaedia's page on review areas could not be opened (section 1d).

**Easily missed, and traps**

- 90% of missed lung cancer diagnoses happen on the chest film. [DELC]
- Miss rate by tumour size in one study it quotes: 29% for 10 mm or less, 28% for 10 to 30 mm, 12% for 30 to 40 mm, none over 40 mm. [DELC]
- Missed cancers are more often in the upper lobes, with ill-defined edges, or hidden by normal structures. [DELC]
- Kinds of reader error: the lesion was never looked at (about 30%), looked at but not recognised (about 25%), or seen and called normal (about 45%). [DELC]
- What helps: compare with old films; use the lateral film; follow a set path; always check the blind areas; spend more than 30 seconds on a film; keep the history in mind. [DELC]
- A pneumothorax is most easily seen at the apex on a PA film and may need a second look. [RM-CS]
- A pneumothorax or haemothorax is easily missed on a supine film. [RCEML]
- Not a pneumothorax: the edge of the scapula, a skin fold, clothing or another artefact. [RCEML]
- Not consolidation: soft tissue shadowing. Not a nodule: a nipple shadow. Not a rib fracture: the junction of a posterior rib and a transverse process. [RCEML]
- Turning the film 90 degrees can make the ribs easier to trace. [RCEML]
- A thin crescent of free gas may be the only sign of perforation. [RM-AB]
- Among Malaysian doctors, pneumomediastinum was correctly identified by 44.4% without a history and 64.3% with one. [KOA]

**Findings the sources treat as needing prompt senior or specialist input**

- Free gas under the diaphragm: bowel perforation is a surgical emergency. Gas after a recent laparoscopy looks the same. [RM-AB] [LITFL-2]
- A widened mediastinum after a deceleration injury (a vehicle crash or a fall from height) can mean aortic rupture. [LITFL-2]
- A suspicious lung lesion: RCEM's worked example is a chest film report that arrives a week later and recommends CT; the department must have a way to act on it. [RCEM-1]
- No source opened gives a list of chest film findings that must go to a senior. See section 5.

### 3.2 General points for limb and joint films

- Views for each joint, from one source: shoulder AP, Y-view and axillary; elbow AP and lateral; wrist with scaphoid views; pelvis AP; ankle AP, lateral and mortise; foot AP, lateral and oblique; cervical spine AP, lateral and open mouth. [UCT]
- A normal film with real clinical concern is managed as an injury with follow-up in several sources: scaphoid [RM-UL] [WIKEM-SCAPH], Lisfranc [RM-LL], hip [RM-LL] [NICE-124], elbow effusion [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB].
- In children a comparison view of the other side is useful because of growth plates [UCT]; one study suggests a comparison view of the uninjured elbow when in doubt [MATT].
- NICE on children: address non-accidental injury before discharge in every child with a femoral fracture, above all a child who is not yet walking or talking. [NICE-38]

### 3.3 Hand and fingers

- When to order: no decision rule for hand films was found. ACR rates radiographs as usually appropriate as the first imaging for acute hand or wrist trauma. [ACR-HW]
- Views: PA, oblique and lateral. [RM-UL]
- Check: each bone in two planes; soft tissue swelling as a clue to a fracture. [RM-UL]
- Fractures into a joint carry a worse outlook and are often the hardest to see. Go through every view, and ask for more views if suspicion is high and nothing is seen. [RM-UL]
- Boxer's fracture is the commonest metacarpal fracture; an oblique one can be subtle. [RM-UL]
- Bennett's fracture: a fracture into the joint at the base of the thumb metacarpal, clearer on the oblique view, usually with subluxation. [RM-UL]
- Mallet finger: an avulsion from the dorsal base of the distal phalanx. A tendon tear without a bone fragment is more common, does not show on the film, and is still a significant injury. [RM-UL]
- A dislocation is usually obvious clinically; the film checks for a fracture with it and confirms the position after reduction. [RM-UL]
- If the first films are negative or unclear and injury is still suspected: repeat films in 10 to 14 days, MRI without contrast, or CT without contrast are each rated usually appropriate. [ACR-HW]
- Hand and finger fractures were 13.1% of all missed fractures, 15.9% in adults. [MATT]

### 3.4 Wrist and scaphoid

- When to order: scaphoid films are indicated after trauma when there is wrist pain with tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. [RM-UL]
- Scaphoid signs listed by WikEM: radial-sided wrist pain, snuffbox tenderness, pain on axial pressure along the thumb metacarpal. [WIKEM-SCAPH]
- The Amsterdam wrist rules (section 4) are a probability model for adults, not a checklist. [AWR]
- Views: PA and lateral. For a suspected scaphoid injury add an oblique view and a PA view in ulnar deviation. [RM-UL]
- Lateral view: check that the radius, lunate and capitate line up. The capitate should sit in the cup of the lunate. [RM-UL]
- Lunate dislocation: the lunate is out of line with the radius. Perilunate dislocation: the capitate is out of line with the lunate. [RM-UL]
- Scapholunate gap: more than 2 mm, or clearly wider than the other gaps between carpal bones, suggests a ligament tear (the Terry Thomas sign). It shows best in ulnar deviation and needs an orthopaedic or hand surgery referral. [RM-UL]
- Distal radius reference values from one paper: radial inclination 23° (range 13 to 30°) on the PA view; radial height 12 mm (range 8 to 18 mm) on the PA view; volar tilt 12° (range 1 to 21°) on the lateral view, with a dorsal tilt counted as negative. The same paper counts a step in the joint surface of 2 mm or more as displaced. [OMAL]
- Easily missed: an occult scaphoid fracture; a triquetral fracture (often seen only on the lateral view, with swelling as a clue); a hamate fracture (often seen only on an oblique view). [RM-UL]
- About 30% of scaphoid fractures are not visible on the first films. [RM-UL] WikEM says up to 10% of first films miss the fracture, and also that about 25% of patients with a negative first film have a fracture. [WIKEM-SCAPH] See section 5.
- If the film is normal and a scaphoid fracture is suspected: treat as a fracture, review, and repeat the films if still tender. [RM-UL]
- Timing of the next step differs by source: MRI if still painful with no fracture seen at 10 days, by local protocol [RM-UL]; repeat films 2 to 3 weeks after injury, MRI being the best test [WIKEM-SCAPH]; consider MRI as the first imaging after a thorough examination [NICE-38]; repeat films in 10 to 14 days, MRI or CT all rated usually appropriate [ACR-HW].
- Why it matters: an untreated scaphoid fracture can fail to unite, with or without avascular necrosis of the proximal fragment. [RM-UL]
- Wrist fractures were 13.1% of all missed fractures and 20.8% of those in children. [MATT]

### 3.5 Elbow, including the child's elbow

- When to order: the elbow extension test (section 4) is the one validated rule found. [APP]
- Views: AP and lateral. [RM-UL] [UCT]
- Check first that the lateral is a true lateral: the distal humerus shows an hourglass or figure-of-eight. Without it the anterior humeral line is not reliable. [DFTB]
- Fat pads: a small anterior fat pad is normal. A raised anterior fat pad (the sail sign) or any visible posterior fat pad means a joint effusion, which after trauma usually means a fracture. [RM-UL] [DFTB] [WIKEM-ELB]
- A visible posterior fat pad is "always" abnormal in two sources [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB] and "almost always" in a third, which adds that it means a fracture in about 75% [DFTB].
- An effusion with no fracture seen: in an adult treat as a radial head fracture; in a child make sure an undisplaced supracondylar fracture or a displaced medial epicondyle is not missed. [WIKEM-ELB] [RM-UL]
- Anterior humeral line: drawn along the front of the humerus on the lateral view. Normal is stated two ways: it passes through the capitellum with at least one third of the capitellum in front of it [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB]; it passes through the middle third of the capitellum [DFTB]. If the capitellum lies behind the line, suspect a supracondylar fracture. [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB]
- Radiocapitellar line: drawn along the centre of the radial neck and head. It should pass through the capitellum. If it does not, suspect a dislocated radial head. [RM-UL] [DFTB] [WIKEM-ELB]
- Check the radiocapitellar line whenever the ulnar shaft is fractured (Monteggia injury). [WIKEM-ELB]
- The line is reliable on a true lateral; on the AP view in a child it can mislead [WIKEM-ELB]. DFTB applies it on both views [DFTB].
- Also look for a slight angulation of the radial neck and a break in the cortex of the distal humerus, front or back. [DFTB]
- Ossification centres, CRITOE (Radiology Masterclass spells it CRITOL): capitellum, radial head, internal (medial) epicondyle, trochlea, olecranon, external (lateral) epicondyle. [RM-UL] [DFTB] [WIKEM-ELB]
- Ages of appearance: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 years, adding 1 year for boys. [WIKEM-ELB] DFTB prints the same numbers without saying they are ages. [DFTB] Radiology Masterclass says only that the centres appear between about 6 months and 12 years. [RM-UL]
- Ages of fusion, one source only: capitellum 12 to 14, radial head 14 to 16, internal epicondyle 16 to 18, trochlea 12 to 14, olecranon 15 to 17, external epicondyle 12 to 14 years. [WIKEM-ELB]
- The key use: the trochlea ossifies after the internal epicondyle. If the trochlea is visible and the internal epicondyle is not where it should be, suspect that it has been pulled off, possibly into the joint. A dislocated elbow can do this. [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB]
- Commonest fractures: radial head in adults (it may show only on the AP view); supracondylar in children. [RM-UL]
- Elbow fractures were 28.6% of missed fractures in children. [MATT]

### 3.6 Shoulder

- When to order: no decision rule was found. ACR rates radiographs as usually appropriate as the first imaging for acute shoulder pain of any cause. [ACR-SH]
- Views: AP and Y-view are the standard trauma views. An axial view can replace the Y-view if the patient can abduct the arm. [RM-UL] The Cape Town book lists AP, Y-view and axillary. [UCT]
- Normal: on the AP the humeral head and glenoid outlines match; on the Y-view the head lies over the glenoid and behind the coracoid. [RM-UL]
- Anterior dislocation (about 50 times more common than posterior): the head lies in front of the glenoid and below the coracoid. [RM-UL]
- Posterior dislocation: linked to seizures and electric shock. On the AP the joint looks widened and the head, held in internal rotation, looks like a light bulb. On the Y-view the head no longer lies over the glenoid. [RM-UL]
- Trap: any film taken in internal rotation can look like the light bulb sign. Use the second view. [RM-UL]
- An uneven cortex on the humeral head after dislocation means an impaction fracture. [RM-UL]
- An adequate AP shoulder film shows from the mid-shaft of the humerus to past the middle of the clavicle. [UCT]
- Shoulder fractures were 10.4% of missed fractures in older adults. [MATT]
- NICE: for a displaced low-energy proximal humerus fracture in an adult, offer non-surgical treatment unless there is an open wound, tenting of the skin, vascular injury, fracture dislocation or a split of the humeral head. [NICE-38]

### 3.7 Foot

- When to order: the Ottawa foot rule (section 4). [NICE-38] [ACR-FOOT]
- If the Ottawa rules are negative but injury outside the midfoot is suspected, foot films are still rated usually appropriate. [ACR-FOOT]
- Views: dorsal-plantar (DP) and oblique; a lateral can help. For a toe alone, DP and oblique of that toe. [RM-LL]
- Lisfranc check on every foot film after injury: on the DP view the 2nd metatarsal lines up with the intermediate cuneiform; on the oblique view the 3rd metatarsal lines up with the lateral cuneiform. [RM-LL]
- A Lisfranc injury can be subtle, is not always displaced, and may not show on the first film. If it is suspected, arrange clinical and X-ray follow-up. [RM-LL]
- Weight-bearing foot films are rated usually appropriate where the Ottawa rules are positive or injury outside the midfoot is suspected. [ACR-FOOT]
- Base of the 5th metatarsal: a fracture line runs across the bone; the normal unfused apophysis in a child runs along it. [RM-LL]
- Metatarsal shaft fractures often show on one view only. [RM-LL]
- Stress fractures are often invisible at first; a later film may show a periosteal reaction; repeat the film or use MRI. [RM-LL]
- Calcaneus: lateral and axial views. Bohler's angle on the lateral view is normally 28 to 40 degrees, and flattens in a severe fracture. [RM-LL]
- A fall from height that breaks the calcaneus can also cause an axial loading fracture of the spine. [RM-LL]
- The foot was the commonest site of missed fractures overall (14.2%), and calcaneal fractures were missed in 14%. [MATT]

### 3.8 Ankle

- When to order: the Ottawa ankle rule (section 4). [NICE-38] [ACR-ANKLE] [BACH]
- ACR: ankle films are usually appropriate when the rule is positive or cannot be applied, and usually not appropriate when it is negative. [ACR-ANKLE]
- Views: AP mortise and lateral. [RM-LL] The Cape Town book lists AP, lateral and mortise. [UCT]
- The joint space should be even all the way round, including both gutters. Widening means instability. [RM-LL] Radiology Masterclass gives no measurement.
- Weber classification of a lateral malleolus fracture: A below the joint line, B at it, C above it. [RM-LL]
- Talar shift: the talus moves sideways with a bimalleolar fracture, widening the medial space, and backwards and sideways with a trimalleolar fracture. [RM-LL]
- Talar dome: inspect its surface on every ankle film after injury. A break in its smooth outline may be the only sign of an osteochondral fracture. [RM-LL]
- Maisonneuve injury: the fibula breaks near the knee, so the ankle film shows only a medial injury or widening. Image the proximal fibula when there is an isolated medial malleolus fracture, joint widening with no fracture seen, a severe ankle injury, or tenderness at the proximal fibula. [RM-LL]
- A ligament injury can leave the ankle unstable with nothing displaced on the film. [RM-LL]
- NICE on follow-up of a single malleolus fracture treated without surgery: weight-bear as tolerated, orthopaedic follow-up within 2 weeks if stability is uncertain, return if not improving at 6 weeks. [NICE-38]
- An adolescent with pain at the front and inner side of the ankle may have a Tillaux fracture and not meet a decision rule. [WIKEM-KNEE]

### 3.9 Knee

- When to order: the Ottawa knee rule, or the Pittsburgh rule (section 4). [NICE-38] [ACR-KNEE]
- Views: at least AP and lateral. [ACR-KNEE] [RM-LL]
- The lateral is taken with the patient supine and a horizontal beam, so that fluid levels show in the suprapatellar pouch. [RM-LL] [ACR-KNEE]
- Lipohaemarthrosis: a fat-on-blood level in the joint. It means fat has leaked from bone, so there is a fracture into the joint even if no line is seen. It may be the only sign of a tibial plateau fracture. [RM-LL] [ACR-KNEE]
- Tibial plateau fractures range from subtle to widely displaced; look for a depressed surface as well as a line. [RM-LL]
- A patellar fracture may show on only one of the two standard views, more often the lateral. A skyline view is for a suspected patellar fracture with normal standard views, or for dislocation. [RM-LL] [ACR-KNEE]
- Normal variants: the fabella (a sesamoid behind the knee) and a bipartite patella. [RM-LL]
- ACR's list of patients in whom the rules should not be used and films should be taken: gross deformity, a palpable mass, a penetrating injury, prosthetic hardware, an unreliable history or examination because of multiple injuries, altered mental state (head injury, drugs or alcohol, dementia), neuropathy (paraplegia, diabetes), or a history that raises the risk of fracture. [ACR-KNEE]
- Children aged 5 to 12 fall outside the age range of both rules, and films may help even without signs. [ACR-KNEE]
- If the films show no fracture and an occult fracture or internal derangement is suspected, MRI without contrast is rated usually appropriate as the next study. [ACR-KNEE]

### 3.10 Hip and pelvis in the older adult

- When to order: no rule that excludes a hip fracture without a film was found. ACR rates hip radiographs as usually appropriate as the first imaging for acute hip pain after trauma. [ACR-HIP]
- Views: AP pelvis and a lateral of the hip. The lateral is often unclear because positioning hurts. [RM-LL]
- Read the whole pelvis on the AP: a pelvic fracture can look clinically like a hip fracture. [RM-LL]
- Shenton's line: the curve of the inner edge of the femoral neck running into the lower edge of the superior pubic ramus. A break in the curve suggests a femoral neck fracture. A normal line does not exclude one. [RM-LL]
- Intracapsular fractures (subcapital, transcervical, basicervical) are the neck of femur fractures. Extracapsular fractures are intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric. [RM-LL]
- The pelvis is a bony ring, and each obturator foramen is a ring too. One break means looking for a second, or for widening of the pubic symphysis or a sacroiliac joint. [RM-AX]
- Pubic ramus fractures are very common in the elderly and usually come in pairs, superior and inferior on the same side. [RM-AX]
- A small step in the pelvic ring cortex can be a fracture running into the acetabulum. [RM-AX]
- Sacrum: check the arcuate lines (the roofs of the sacral foramina) on every pelvic film. A broken curve suggests a sacral fracture. [RM-AX]
- The extent of a pelvic injury is often underestimated on plain films. [RM-AX]
- Young athletes: avulsion fractures, most often of the anterior superior iliac spine. An unfused iliac crest apophysis in a teenager is normal. [RM-AX]
- Not all hip fractures show on the first film. An undisplaced fracture may also lack the short, externally rotated leg. Take particular care with acutely confused patients. [RM-LL]
- If the film is normal and a fracture is still suspected: NICE offers MRI, and considers CT if MRI is not available within 24 hours or is contraindicated [NICE-124]; ACR rates MRI and CT without contrast both as usually appropriate [ACR-HIP]; Radiology Masterclass says repeat films, CT or MRI [RM-LL]; the Dutch study advises MRI or CT [MATT].
- In older adults the pelvis and hip were 37.3% of missed fractures. Ten of 1,450 femoral neck fractures (0.7%) and 7 of 38 acetabular fractures (18%) were missed. [MATT]
- NICE: assess safeguarding, comorbidities and falls risk at the first presentation of a fracture. [NICE-38]

### 3.11 Cervical spine

- Whether the plain film is a first-line test at all is the main disagreement in this file. See section 5.
- Who needs imaging: the Canadian C-spine rule or NEXUS (section 4). [NICE-41] [ACR-SPINE]
- NICE, adults (16 and over): if the Canadian C-spine rule indicates imaging, do CT. After a head injury with neck pain or tenderness, do CT within 1 hour if neck movement cannot be assessed safely, if the patient cannot actively rotate the neck 45 degrees each way, or if a condition such as axial spondyloarthritis raises the risk. [NICE-41] [NICE-232]
- NICE, under 16: three-view cervical spine films within 1 hour in the lower-risk groups it lists, read by a clinician trained to interpret them; discuss the films with a consultant radiologist and image further if needed; a peg view may be left out if the child cannot follow commands or open the mouth; CT if the films are inadequate, show a significant bony injury, or strong suspicion remains despite normal films. [NICE-232] [NICE-41]
- ACR, 16 and over: cervical spine radiographs are rated usually not appropriate as the first imaging, whether or not the rules indicate imaging; CT without contrast is rated usually appropriate when they do. [ACR-SPINE]
- NICE: images for spinal injury should be read at once by a professional trained to do so. [NICE-41]
- Radiology Masterclass teaches the film for emergency clinicians, with these warnings: a normal film does not exclude a significant injury; experienced clinicians should lead the assessment; CT or MRI is often right for a high-risk injury, a neurological deficit, a limited examination or unclear films. [RM-AX]
- Views: lateral (often the most informative), AP and open-mouth peg view. [RM-AX] [UCT]
- Adequacy: the lateral must show from the skull base to the top of T2; down to T1 is accepted. If T1 is not shown, repeat with the arms pulled down or take a swimmer's view; CT may be needed if the junction is still not seen. [RM-AX]
- Reading order: coverage; alignment; bones; spacing; soft tissues; edge of the image. [RM-AX]
- Alignment on the lateral: three smooth lines. The anterior line along the front of the vertebral bodies, the posterior line along the back of the bodies, and the spinolaminar line along the front of the spinous processes. [RM-AX]
- Bones: trace the cortex of every bone and check the height of each body; check the ring of C2 on the lateral for a step; check each spinous process. [RM-AX]
- Spacing: discs, and the gaps between spinous processes. [RM-AX]
- Prevertebral soft tissue: no more than one third of a vertebral body width above C4, and no more than a full body width below C4. Thickening can be the only sign of a fracture. Normal thickness does not reassure. [RM-AX]
- Peg view: the lateral masses of C1 line up with C2; the gaps on each side of the peg are equal (head rotation can make them unequal with normal alignment). The peg is often hidden by teeth or the occiput. [RM-AX]
- AP view: the lateral edges line up; the spinous processes are in a straight line and evenly spaced; look for surgical emphysema, the upper ribs and the lung apices. [RM-AX]
- Injuries named: Jefferson fracture of C1; peg fracture (often clearer on the lateral); hangman fracture of C2; extension and flexion teardrop fractures (the flexion type is highly unstable); perched facets (may show no fracture, with a high rate of cord injury); clay-shoveller's fracture of a spinous process. [RM-AX]
- Easily missed: spinous process fractures at the cervicothoracic junction, peg fractures, injuries showing only soft tissue thickening, and fractures seen only on CT. [RM-AX]
- If a peg fracture is suspected clinically and not seen, a senior clinician may consider CT. [RM-AX]
- NICE on the thoracic and lumbar spine: X-ray first for a suspected column injury from T1 to L3 without abnormal neurology; CT if the film is abnormal or signs of a column injury are present; if a new fracture is confirmed, image the rest of the spine. [NICE-41]

### 3.12 Abdominal X-ray

**When it is indicated**

- The main indications are suspected bowel obstruction and perforation, and for perforation the film to ask for is the erect chest film. [RM-AB]
- RCEMLearning: do it for acute abdominal pain if small or large bowel obstruction is suspected, and for a flare of inflammatory bowel disease to exclude toxic megacolon. It still has a role in foreign body ingestion or insertion. [RCEML]
- CT is increasingly replacing the abdominal film for obstruction, because it shows the cause and other diagnoses. [RCEML]
- The RCR audit template lists the iRefer indications (version 8.0.1, May 2017): clinical suspicion of obstruction; acute flare of inflammatory bowel disease; palpable mass (specific circumstances); constipation (specific circumstances); acute and chronic pancreatitis (specific circumstances); sharp or poisonous foreign body; smooth and small foreign body such as a coin or battery (specific circumstances); blunt or stab abdominal injury (specific circumstances). Its target is that 100% of emergency department requests meet these. [RCR-2]
- A 2022 audit lists the same, with one more: a follow-through film after Gastrografin. [HASSAN]
- A 2012 paper lists an older RCR set: acute abdominal pain with obstruction or perforation; suspected oesophageal perforation; small bowel obstruction; large bowel obstruction; inflammatory bowel disease, to find toxic megacolon; acute pancreatitis only where pain is non-specific, to exclude other causes. [LOUGH]
- iRefer itself could not be read (section 1d).

**When it is not**

- It is not the film for free gas. The erect chest film is the most sensitive plain film for that. [RM-AB] [RCEML]
- None of the lists above includes undifferentiated abdominal pain, and one paper describes films for that as an old routine that the RCR lists restrict. [HASSAN]
- Dose: one abdominal film is 0.7 mSv, about 35 chest films or four months of background radiation. [RCEML]
- The film gives limited information. Normal structures can hide disease, for example a small renal stone behind bowel. A poor film can rarely be improved by repeating it. [RM-AB]

**Views and adequacy**

- The standard film is a supine AP. A decubitus film is for a patient who cannot sit or stand, and is rarely done. [RM-AB]
- If perforation is suspected, the patient should sit upright for 10 to 20 minutes before the erect chest film. As little as 1 ml of gas can then show. [RM-AB]
- Coverage: the diaphragm to the hernial orifices, and both flanks [RM-AB]; including the properitoneal fat stripe at each side [RCEML]. Two films are often needed. [RM-AB]
- Check the name, date of birth, date and side markers. [RCEML] [RM-AB]

**Reading order**

- Radiology Masterclass: patient details; quality; then bowel gas, soft tissues, bones and abnormal calcification. [RM-AB]
- RCEMLearning: data; adequacy; bowels; calcification; abnormal air; hernial orifices; bony skeleton. [RCEML]

**Normal values**

- Upper limits of bowel diameter, the 3/6/9 rule: small bowel 3 cm, colon 6 cm, caecum 9 cm. [RM-AB]
- Small bowel is central and has valvulae conniventes, thin folds that cross the whole width. [RM-AB]
- Large bowel is at the edges, has haustra, and holds mottled faeces. [RM-AB]

**Findings**

- Small bowel obstruction: several dilated gas-filled loops in the centre, over 3 cm. The longer the dilated length, the more likely a true obstruction. Ileus can look the same. Examine the hernial orifices and ask about past surgery. [RM-AB]
- Large bowel obstruction: dilated colon down to a level, with little or no gas beyond it. The film cannot reliably tell mechanical obstruction from pseudo-obstruction. The commonest causes are colorectal cancer and diverticular stricture. [RM-AB]
- Free gas on a supine film: Rigler's sign (both sides of the bowel wall visible); the football sign (a large round lucency); gas outlining the falciform ligament or the liver edge; triangles of gas between loops. [RM-AB]
- A sentinel loop is a single dilated small bowel loop beside an inflamed organ, for example in pancreatitis. It is not diagnostic. [RM-AB]

**Traps**

- Phleboliths taken for renal stones. [RCEML]
- A gastric bubble or bowel gas taken for free gas. [RCEML]
- Faecal loading read as constipation. A loaded caecum is normal. [RCEML]

**Urgent**

- Bowel perforation is a surgical emergency. [RM-AB]
- Suspected obstruction or perforation should have CT expedited. [LOUGH]

## 4. Decision rules for imaging

Restated, not quoted. The original papers were not open to read (section 1d), so each rule is given as the named secondary sources state it, and their differences are noted.

### 4.1 Ottawa ankle rule and Ottawa foot rule

- Ankle films are needed only if there is pain in the malleolar zone and any one of: bone tenderness along the distal 6 cm of the posterior edge of the fibula or at the tip of the lateral malleolus; bone tenderness along the distal 6 cm of the posterior edge of the tibia or at the tip of the medial malleolus; unable to bear weight for four steps both straight after the injury and in the emergency department. [NHSS] [WP-ANK] [MDCALC]
- Foot films are needed only if there is pain in the midfoot and any one of: bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal; bone tenderness at the navicular; unable to bear weight for four steps both straight after the injury and in the emergency department. [NHSS] [WP-ANK] [MDCALC]
- One paper also lists the cuboid among the midfoot sites, and age 55 or over as a criterion. No other source read does. [JENKIN]
- Who endorses it: NICE says use the Ottawa ankle and foot rules to decide on an X-ray in people over 5 years with a suspected ankle fracture. [NICE-38] ACR builds its ankle and foot ratings on the rule. [ACR-ANKLE] [ACR-FOOT]
- Validation: a systematic review of 32 studies (27 studies and 15,581 patients in the meta-analysis). Pooled sensitivity 97.6%; 99.6% when applied within 48 hours of injury. Negative likelihood ratio 0.08 for the ankle, 0.08 for the midfoot and 0.07 in children. It should cut unnecessary films by 30 to 40%. [BACH]
- The multicentre trial that introduced the rule covered 12,777 adults in eight Canadian emergency departments. Ankle films fell from 82.8% to 60.9% of patients, with no rise in fractures found after discharge (0.5% against 0.4%). [STIELL-95]
- Children: 98.5% sensitive in the paediatric population, per WikEM; supported in children over 6, with less known in younger children, per Wikipedia. [WIKEM-ANK] [WP-ANK]
- Exclusions differ by source:
  - NHS Scotland sheet: under 18; intoxication; multiple painful injuries; pregnancy; head injury; reduced sensation from a neurological deficit. [NHSS]
  - A 2010 summary, attributing them to Stiell: injury more than 10 days old; pregnancy; isolated skin injuries; under 18. [JENKIN]
  - WikEM: age under 6 or over 55; blunt trauma only; not for subacute or chronic injuries; not for the hindfoot or forefoot. [WIKEM-ANK]
  - ACR: examples only, a neurological disorder or neuropathy. [ACR-ANKLE]
  - Wikipedia: pregnancy, and patients who cannot follow the test, for example with a head injury or intoxication. [WP-ANK]
- It is a one-way rule: sensitive, not specific. If no criterion is met, a film may still be taken on clinical judgement. [MDCALC]
- Original papers (citations from Europe PMC, text not read): Stiell IG, Greenberg GH, McKnight RD, et al. A study to develop clinical decision rules for the use of radiography in acute ankle injuries. Ann Emerg Med 1992;21(4):384-390. Stiell IG, Greenberg GH, McKnight RD, et al. Decision rules for the use of radiography in acute ankle injuries. Refinement and prospective validation. JAMA 1993;269(9):1127-1132. Stiell IG, McKnight RD, Greenberg GH, et al. Implementation of the Ottawa ankle rules. JAMA 1994;271(11):827-832. [EPMC]

### 4.2 Ottawa knee rule

- Knee films are needed after an acute knee injury only if any one of: age 55 or over; tenderness at the head of the fibula; isolated tenderness of the patella; cannot flex the knee to 90 degrees; cannot bear weight for four steps both straight after the injury and in the emergency department. [WP-KNEE] [ACR-KNEE]
- WikEM prints the age as over 55. [WIKEM-KNEE] ACR's rating text also says "over 55" in one place and "55 years of age or older" in its list. [ACR-KNEE]
- Age range: the ACR narrative applies the rule to patients of 18 years (the sign before the number did not survive in the PDF text; check the PDF) and says children of 5 to 12 fall outside both knee rules. [ACR-KNEE] NICE says use the rule in people over 2 years. [NICE-38] WikEM says it can be applied to children and is 99% sensitive over age 5. [WIKEM-KNEE] See section 5.
- Accuracy: sensitivity 98.5% and specificity 48.6% [WIKEM-KNEE]; 98 to 100% sensitivity in some studies, with poor specificity [WP-KNEE].
- Do not apply the rule, and take films, in the groups ACR lists (section 3.9). [ACR-KNEE]
- Original papers (citations from Europe PMC, text not read): Stiell IG, Greenberg GH, Wells GA, et al. Derivation of a decision rule for the use of radiography in acute knee injuries. Ann Emerg Med 1995;26(4):405-413. Stiell IG, Greenberg GH, Wells GA, et al. Prospective validation of a decision rule for the use of radiography in acute knee injuries. JAMA 1996;275(8):611-615. Stiell IG, Wells GA, Hoag RH, et al. Implementation of the Ottawa Knee Rule for the use of radiography in acute knee injuries. JAMA 1997;278(23):2075-2079. [EPMC]

### 4.3 Pittsburgh knee rule

- After blunt trauma or a fall, knee films are needed if the patient is under 12 or over 50, or cannot walk four weight-bearing steps in the emergency department. [WP-PITT] [ACR-KNEE]
- Only a full heel-and-toe step counts, where the Ottawa rules also count a limping step. [WP-PITT]
- Reported accuracy: sensitivity 99%, specificity 60%. [WP-PITT] In one study of 106 patients, both rules found every fracture; Ottawa would have avoided 25% of films and Pittsburgh 30%. [ACR-KNEE]
- Original paper (citation from Wikipedia, text not read): Seaberg DC, Jackson R. Clinical decision rule for knee radiographs. Am J Emerg Med 1994;12(5):541-543. [WP-PITT]

### 4.4 Canadian C-spine rule

- As NICE states it: high risk with any one of: age 65 or over; a dangerous mechanism (a fall from more than 1 metre or 5 steps, an axial load to the head such as diving, a high-speed or rollover crash, ejection from a vehicle, a motorised recreational vehicle accident, a bicycle collision, a horse-riding accident); paraesthesia in the arms or legs. [NICE-41]
- Low risk with any one of: a minor rear-end vehicle collision; comfortable sitting; walking at any time since the injury; no midline cervical tenderness; delayed onset of neck pain. [NICE-41]
- Neck movement may be tested only in a low-risk patient with no high-risk factor. Able to rotate the neck actively 45 degrees to the left and right: no risk, and no imaging or immobilisation. Unable: still at risk, and image. [NICE-41]
- NICE notes that the rule is hard to apply in children. [NICE-41]
- Derived in 8,924 alert and stable adults in ten Canadian emergency departments (sensitivity 100%, specificity 42.5% on cross-validation). In a later study in nine Canadian departments it was more sensitive and more specific than NEXUS. [EPMC]
- The exclusions in the original paper were not read. The Malaysian head injury CPG in the app prints the rule as its Appendix 6 (section 6).
- Original papers (citations from Europe PMC, text not read): Stiell IG, Wells GA, Vandemheen KL, et al. The Canadian C-spine rule for radiography in alert and stable trauma patients. JAMA 2001;286(15):1841-1848. Stiell IG, Clement CM, McKnight RD, et al. The Canadian C-spine rule versus the NEXUS low-risk criteria in patients with trauma. N Engl J Med 2003;349(26):2510-2518. [EPMC]

### 4.5 NEXUS low-risk criteria

- After blunt trauma, imaging of the cervical spine is not needed only if all five hold: no midline cervical tenderness; no focal neurological deficit; normal alertness; no intoxication; no painful distracting injury. [WIKEM-NEXUS] [WP-CSP]
- Validated in 34,069 patients with blunt trauma at 21 centres in the United States; it found 99% of cervical injuries, with low specificity. [EPMC] WikEM gives 99.6% sensitivity for a clinically important injury and 12.9% specificity. [WIKEM-NEXUS]
- Caution in older patients: only 8.6% of the original study's patients were elderly, and the rule takes no account of age. [WIKEM-NEXUS]
- Original paper (citation from Europe PMC, text not read): Hoffman JR, Mower WR, Wolfson AB, et al. Validity of a set of clinical criteria to rule out injury to the cervical spine in patients with blunt trauma. N Engl J Med 2000;343(2):94-99. [EPMC]

### 4.6 Elbow extension test

- The test: the patient sits, lifts both arms forward to 90 degrees at the shoulder, then fully straightens and locks both elbows. Compare the two sides by eye. [APP]
- Studied in adults (over 15) and children (3 to 15) within 72 hours of an elbow injury, at five emergency departments in south-west England. [APP]
- Excluded: limited extension before the injury; altered mental state; multiple injuries; no history of trauma; injury more than 72 hours old; neuromuscular disease; suspected intentional injury; osteogenesis imperfecta. [APP]
- Results: adults, sensitivity 98.4%, specificity 47.7%, negative predictive value 98.4%; five fractures in adults with full extension, two of them olecranon fractures that needed an operation. Children, sensitivity 94.6%, specificity 49.5%, negative predictive value 95.8%; twelve fractures with full extension, none needing an operation. [APP]
- The authors' advice: cannot fully extend, take films (nearly half have a fracture). Can fully extend, films may be deferred if the clinician is confident there is no olecranon fracture; be cautious in children; the patient returns if symptoms have not settled in 7 to 10 days. [APP]
- Paper: Appelboam A, Reuben AD, Benger JR, et al. BMJ 2008;337:a2428. [APP]

### 4.7 Amsterdam wrist rules

- A probability formula for adults with wrist pain or tenderness after trauma, not a checklist. A cut-off turns the probability into a yes or no for films: 21% for any wrist fracture, 4% for a distal radius fracture. The authors planned a phone app to do the sum. [AWR]
- Inputs for the distal radius model: age; wrist swelling; visible deformity; distal radius tenderness; pain on palmar flexion; pain on supination; pain on ulnar deviation; pain on the radioulnar ballottement test. [AWR]
- Inputs for the all-fracture model: age; male sex; wrist swelling; swelling of the anatomical snuffbox; visible deformity; distal radius tenderness; pain on radial deviation; pain on axial compression of the thumb. [AWR]
- Studied in 882 adults at five Dutch hospitals. Excluded: injury more than 72 hours old; multitrauma (Injury Severity Score 16 or more); a wrist film already taken. [AWR]
- External validation: any fracture, sensitivity 98.2%, specificity 21.0%; distal radius, sensitivity 98.4%, specificity 25.1%. Films would have fallen by 10.4%. Four fractures (1.6%) would have been missed, two of them scaphoid. [AWR]
- The authors advise caution until an implementation study reports. [AWR] Europe PMC lists that study (Mulders MAM, et al. Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg 2020;46(3):573-582); it was not read. [EPMC]
- Paper: Walenkamp MMJ, et al. BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2015;16:389. [AWR]

## 5. Where sources disagree, and where nothing was found

### Disagreements

1. **Cervical spine film in adults.** NICE (2016 and 2023) and ACR (2024) send adults who need imaging to CT, and ACR rates the plain film usually not appropriate. [NICE-41] [NICE-232] [ACR-SPINE] NICE keeps three-view films for children under 16. [NICE-232] Radiology Masterclass still teaches the three views to emergency clinicians, with heavy caveats. [RM-AX] The Malaysian head injury CPG (2015) in the app, adapted from NICE 2014, uses three-view films within 1 hour for lower-risk adults (section 6). So the app's present national source and the current NICE guidance differ. This is a question for the department: is the lateral cervical spine film a Green Zone film at all?
2. **Chest film inspiration.** Five sources give five counts: 5 to 7 anterior ribs; 5 to 6 anterior or 8 to 10 posterior; 10th or 11th rib posteriorly and the 6th costal cartilage anteriorly; the posterior 9th rib; 9 to 10 posterior ribs. [RM-CQ] [LITFL-1] [LITFL-2] [RCEML] [WP-CXR] The two LITFL pages disagree with each other.
3. **Ottawa ankle rule exclusions and age.** Under 18 excluded [NHSS] [JENKIN]; under 6 or over 55 excluded [WIKEM-ANK]; use in people over 5 years [NICE-38]. One source adds the cuboid and age 55 or over to the criteria. [JENKIN] The original exclusions were not read.
4. **Ottawa knee rule age.** 55 or over [WP-KNEE] [ACR-KNEE] against over 55 [WIKEM-KNEE]. Lower age limit: adults per the ACR narrative [ACR-KNEE]; over 2 years per NICE [NICE-38]; children over 5 per WikEM [WIKEM-KNEE].
5. **Scaphoid.** How many are missed on the first film: about 30% [RM-UL]; up to 10%, and also about 25% of those with a negative film, on the same page [WIKEM-SCAPH]. What next: MRI first [NICE-38]; MRI at 10 days if still painful [RM-UL]; repeat films at 2 to 3 weeks [WIKEM-SCAPH]; repeat films at 10 to 14 days, MRI or CT, all acceptable [ACR-HW].
6. **Occult hip fracture.** MRI first, CT if MRI is not available within 24 hours [NICE-124]; MRI or CT equally [ACR-HIP]; repeat films, CT or MRI [RM-LL]. The Malaysian hip fracture CPG in the app calls CT the modality of choice locally (section 6).
7. **Anterior humeral line.** At least one third of the capitellum in front of the line [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB] against through the middle third [DFTB].
8. **Posterior fat pad.** Always abnormal [RM-UL] [WIKEM-ELB] against almost always [DFTB].
9. **Radiocapitellar line on the AP view in a child.** Use it on both views [DFTB] against it can mislead on the AP [WIKEM-ELB].
10. **Rule of twos.** The fifth "two" is a comparison radiograph in one version and a second opinion in the other; "two occasions" means a follow-up film in one and before and after reduction in the other. [LITFL-3] [UCT] A 1995 BMJ letter calling the rule outmoded turned up in a search but was not opened.
11. **Abdominal film indications.** The lists differ with the iRefer version: constipation, palpable mass and pancreatitis appear "in specific circumstances" in the 2017 list and not in the 2012 paper's list. [RCR-2] [HASSAN] [LOUGH] The current iRefer edition was not read.
12. **Hot reporting.** NICE wants a definitive report before the patient leaves [NICE-38]; RCEM says the service is absent or limited in many departments and out of hours, and sets a 72-hour review of abnormal reports as the standard instead [RCEM-1].
13. **Right hemidiaphragm.** Higher by 1 to 3 cm [LITFL-2] or about 2.5 cm [LITFL-1]; RCEMLearning gives no figure [RCEML].
